Hurricane Season Prep for Winter Haven Homes: Windows, Attics and Storm Damage

Published 12 August 2026 · Insulflo Energy Services, Lakeland FL

Mid-August through October is the busiest stretch of the Atlantic hurricane season, and Winter Haven sits far enough inland that a lot of homeowners quietly assume it is someone else’s problem. Polk County has learned otherwise more than once. Inland does not mean untouched — it means the wind arrives with less warning in the forecast conversation, and the damage is often less obvious afterward.

The good news is that most of what protects a house here is not dramatic. It is a handful of checks you can do in an afternoon, plus knowing which problems are worth calling someone about before the sky turns green.

Start with the windows, because they fail first

Windows are the weak point in almost every Florida storm. Once a window goes, wind gets inside the house and pushes up against the roof from below at the same time it is pulling from above. That is the failure mode that turns a bad afternoon into a rebuild.

Walk the outside of your home and look for four things. Glazing putty or sealant that has gone chalky, cracked or is missing in sections. Frames that have swollen, rotted at the corners, or no longer sit square. Windows that stick, rattle in the frame, or need a shove to latch. And any pane with a crack, chip or fogging between the glass layers — fogging means the seal has already failed.

Older single-pane windows, common in Winter Haven’s mid-century neighborhoods, were not built to today’s wind-borne debris standards. If yours are original to the house, that is worth knowing before a storm rather than after. Modern impact-rated window installation is a meaningful upgrade in a wind zone, and it does double duty on your cooling bill the rest of the year.

Then go up into the attic

Your attic tells you things the rest of the house hides. Go up on a bright day, turn the lights off, and look at the roof deck.

Daylight coming through anywhere — a seam, a nail hole, around a vent boot — is a path for water. Dark streaks or stains on the underside of the deck mean water has been getting in already, storm or no storm. Soft or spongy sheathing means it has been going on a while. Rusted nail tips, damp insulation, or a musty smell all point the same direction.

Check the insulation itself while you are up there. If it is matted flat, visibly thin between the joists, or you can see the tops of the framing across the whole attic, there is not much left doing its job. Current guidance for our climate zone is roughly R-30 to R-60 in the attic, which for loose fill is a good deal deeper than most people expect. Blown-in insulation is the usual fix, and it can often be topped up over what is there.

One caveat: insulation that has been wet does not recover. It compresses, holds moisture against the wood, and can grow mold. Wet or contaminated material needs insulation removal before anything new goes down. Adding fresh insulation on top of soaked insulation just hides the problem.

The week before a storm

When a system is actually tracking toward Central Florida, the useful list is short. Clear gutters and downspouts so water leaves the roofline instead of backing up under the shingles. Trim branches that overhang the roof. Bring in or tie down anything in the yard that can become a projectile — patio furniture, grills, planters, the trampoline. Photograph the outside of your house and the inside of each room, and put the photos somewhere that is not your kitchen counter. That five-minute step is what makes an insurance claim straightforward later.

If you have storm shutters or panels, confirm the hardware is all present and that you can actually install them, in daylight, before you need to do it in a hurry.

After the storm, look before you assume

Most storm damage in an inland county is not a roof torn off. It is subtle: lifted shingles, a displaced vent boot, water tracking down inside a wall, insulation that got soaked through a small opening and is now sitting wet over your ceiling.

Go back into the attic within a few days of the weather clearing and repeat the same daylight-and-stains check. Look at the ceilings in each room for new discoloration. Check the windows for water intrusion at the sills, and for frames that have shifted. If you find wet insulation, it needs to come out sooner rather than later — drying in place does not really happen in Florida humidity, and what starts as a damp patch becomes a mold question in a couple of weeks.

Worth doing before the season peaks

None of this needs to happen all at once. If you do one thing this month, go up and look at your attic — it is free, it takes twenty minutes, and it tells you whether you are in good shape or have a real gap. Windows are the bigger project, and worth planning rather than rushing.
