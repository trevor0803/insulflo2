Why Your Upstairs Won't Cool in Tampa — and What Actually Fixes It

Published 19 August 2026 · Insulflo Energy Services, Lakeland FL

It is the most common complaint we hear from Tampa homeowners, and it is almost always the same story. Downstairs is fine. Upstairs is four to eight degrees warmer no matter what the thermostat says. The system runs and runs. Somebody has already been out to look at the air conditioner and found nothing wrong with it.

There usually is nothing wrong with it. The problem is not the equipment. It is where the equipment lives.

Your ductwork is sitting in a 130-degree attic

In most Tampa homes built in the last thirty years, the air handler and the ducts that feed the upstairs rooms are in the attic. On an August afternoon that attic is running 30 to 50 degrees above the outside air. Call it 130 degrees under dark shingles.

So the system cools air to around 55 degrees, then pushes it through a hundred feet of duct surrounded by 130-degree air. By the time it reaches the bedroom furthest from the unit it has warmed considerably. Every joint that leaks is leaking cold air into the attic and pulling hot attic air in behind it.

The upstairs rooms are the ones at the end of that run. That is why they lose and the downstairs wins.

Why adding more insulation to the attic floor does not fix it

This is the part that costs people money. The instinct is to pile more insulation on the attic floor, and a contractor will happily sell that. It is not a bad thing to do — it will reduce heat coming through the ceiling — but it does nothing at all for the ducts. Insulating the attic floor keeps the attic outside the conditioned part of your house. Your ductwork is still up there, in the heat, on the wrong side of the insulation.

If your upstairs problem is duct-driven, a deeper layer of attic insulation will improve things slightly and leave you disappointed for the money.

What actually fixes it: moving the boundary

The fix is to insulate the underside of the roof deck instead of the attic floor, and seal the attic at the roofline. That brings the entire attic — ductwork, air handler and all — inside the conditioned envelope of the house.

The attic stops being a 130-degree oven and settles closer to indoor temperature. The ducts are no longer fighting anything. The air arriving in the far bedroom is close to the temperature it left at. Most homeowners describe the change as the upstairs finally "keeping up" rather than the house feeling colder.

It also means the air handler is no longer working in extreme heat, which is easier on equipment that is expensive to replace.

We do this with spray foam applied at the roofline. Open-cell is what we specify for most Tampa attics — it is vapour-permeable, so the roof deck can still dry inward, which matters in this climate.

How to tell which problem you have

Before you spend anything, a few checks will tell you a lot.

  • Is the difference worst in the rooms furthest from the air handler? That points at ducts, not ceiling heat.
  • Does it stay hot upstairs well into the evening, after the sun is off the roof? That is heat stored in the structure and the attic, and it points at the attic as a whole.
  • Go up and look. If you can see the ducts running through open attic space, and especially if any are crushed, disconnected at a boot, or sagging between supports, you have found part of your answer.
  • Look at the insulation depth. If you can see the tops of the ceiling joists across the whole attic, there is not much left doing its job either way.

It is common to have both problems. A house with thin, settled insulation and ducts in a hot attic needs the whole assembly addressed, not one half of it.

Is it always the right answer?

No, and we will tell you when it is not.

If your roof is near the end of its life, seal the attic after the roof is replaced, not before. If your ductwork is genuinely damaged — crushed runs, disconnected boots — that is a duct repair, and no amount of insulation compensates for air dumping into the attic. And if your attic is simply under-insulated with a functioning single-storey layout, a blown-in top-up is a fraction of the cost and may be all you need.

If the existing material is wet, compacted or contaminated, it has to come out first — foam has to bond to a clean deck, and insulation removal is step one on more Tampa jobs than people expect, particularly in homes that have taken storm water.

What it costs

We will not quote a square-foot figure that changes the moment we see your attic. The variables that actually move the price are the area being covered, the depth and type of foam specified, how much old material has to come out first, how accessible the space is, and whether it is a straightforward attic or a cut-up roofline with dormers and knee walls. The estimate is free and done in person, which is the only way to give you a number that holds.

Worth doing before next summer, not during it

August is when people call, which is understandable and also the worst time to be making the decision. Attic work in a 130-degree space takes longer and schedules are tight. If you can live with one more month of it, autumn is a considerably easier time to get the job done properly.
